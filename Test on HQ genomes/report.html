Growth Rate Predictions for the High Quality GEM Genomes

GEM-WorldModel project, version 3, October 2026

This report covers predicted growth rates for the 9,143 high quality genomes in the GEM catalog, genomes that have never had a growth rate measured in a lab. We produced a prediction for 9,139 of them. This version replaces the first one, which should not be used. The next section explains why.

What was wrong with the first version

The first predictions gave the model information about where each organism sits on the tree of life, and about its 16S gene, in a different numerical form from the one the model had been trained on. The model still produced numbers, but they were squeezed toward fast growth: the median predicted doubling time was 0.6 hours against 7 hours for the species it was trained on, no genome was predicted to take longer than 24 hours, and gut microbes looked no different from ocean microbes. We found this while rebuilding the pipeline. It is fixed, and the prediction script now stops if its inputs fall outside the range of the training data.

What the table contains

The file hq_genome_predictions.csv has one row per genome. The main columns are:

  • doubling_time_hours_at_growth_temp, the predicted doubling time, in hours, at the temperature the organism itself grows best at. This is what you would measure in a culture at its own optimum. range_low_hours and range_high_hours give a range that covered about 80% of held-out species, and typical_fold_error is how far off a prediction typically is, as a multiplier.
  • predicted_growth_temp_c, the predicted growth temperature in degrees C, and hot_organism, true when that is 45 C or above.
  • doubling_time_hours_at_20C, the same doubling time rescaled to 20 C so organisms that grow at very different temperatures can be compared. For hot-growing organisms this is an extrapolation to a temperature they could not grow at, so it should be read as a relative score and not a prediction of real growth.
  • nearest_labeled_relative, model_used and phylogeny_rank_used, which say how close the genome is to a species with a measured growth rate and which inputs the prediction could use.
  • The genome traits, codon_usage_bias, ivywrel_fraction, assembly quality (completeness, contamination), taxonomy and habitat from the GEM catalog.

How the prediction works

Five kinds of information are taken from each genome's DNA: its size, GC content and number of tRNA genes; how strongly its ribosomal protein genes favor particular codons (codon usage bias, a known signature of fast growth); the share of its proteins made of the amino acids I, V, Y, W, R, E and L, which rises with growth temperature; where it sits on the tree of life; and its 16S gene. Genes were predicted directly from the DNA, so no annotation was needed, and the same steps were applied to the species with measured growth rates and to the GEM genomes.

A model was trained on 597 genomes from 509 species with a measured growth rate (498 bacterial genomes and 99 archaeal genomes), mostly from the compilation by Madin and colleagues (2020). It predicts three things: the doubling time at the organism's own growth temperature, the growth temperature itself, and the doubling time rescaled to 20 C. Growth temperature matters because rescaling multiplies a hot-growing organism's doubling time by a large factor, about 4 for organisms growing at 30 to 45 C, 16 at 45 to 60 C and 48 at 60 to 80 C, so a model can look accurate on the rescaled value partly by recognizing hot organisms. We therefore report both values, and judge the model mainly on the first.

Not every genome had every input. 7,297 genomes had all four and got the full model. The rest were predicted with whichever inputs they had, and the table says which. For 10% of genomes the nearest labeled relative is in the same genus, for 22% in the same family, for 26% in the same order, and 41% have no labeled relative at order level. These shares are worst cases, because GEM uses older taxonomic names than the labeled species and a renamed group looks more distant than it is.

How accurate it is

Accuracy was never measured on a species the model had seen. We also left out whole genera, families and orders, because most GEM genomes have no close relative among the species with measured rates, and accuracy falls as the nearest relative gets more distant. In plain terms, a typical prediction at the organism's own temperature is off by a factor of about 1.9 when a genus relative is known and about 2.4 when none is, and the ranking of genomes from fast to slow is moderately reliable, better for close relatives. The predictions are best used to rank genomes and to find groups that are likely fast or slow, not as exact doubling times.

Two further checks. First, we trained on the original species only and predicted 188 bacterial species it had never seen: typical error 2.3x at the organism's own temperature (ranking 0.51) and 2.0x for the 20 C value (ranking 0.75). Second, because the GEM genomes are assembled from environmental samples and not isolates, we checked the pipeline on 718 real GEM genomes from 48 species that have a measured growth rate, each species held out of training. Typical error was 2.5x from the GEM genomes against 1.9x from the isolate genomes of the same species (this check used the model without 16S so that more species qualify), so assembling genomes from the environment costs some accuracy, while the ranking is almost unchanged.

The model compresses the range. Among the held-out species, 7% have a measured doubling time above 24 hours but only 1.7% are predicted to, and a tenfold change in the measured time shows up as about a 2.5-fold change in the prediction. It can tell that a very slow species is slower than most (it separates species above 24 hours from the rest with an area under the curve of 0.79) but not how slow it is. Read the predictions as a relative score, and expect very slow growth to be underestimated.

Weak spots. Ranking among fast growers (doubling time under 5 hours) is the weakest part. Codon usage bias helped most there, but it is still only moderate. Archaea are covered by far fewer labeled species than bacteria, mostly hyperthermophiles and methanogens, and groups with no labeled archaeal relative are less reliable than the numbers above.

What came out

The predicted doubling time at the organism's own growth temperature has a median of 3.1 hours (middle 90% from 0.7 to 10), against 2.2 hours (middle 90% from 0.3 to 32) measured for the labeled species. The spread is much narrower for the reason given above: when the model is unsure it pulls the prediction toward the middle, so the spread of the predictions understates the spread of real growth rates. About 7% of genomes are predicted to grow at 45 C or above. As a check on the growth temperature, which the model was not told anything about, 66% of the 396 genomes from hot spring and other thermal habitats are predicted to grow at 45 C or above, against 1% of the human-associated genomes. The second number is not zero, so a few mesophiles are being called hot. Treat the hot_organism flag as a hint, not a measurement.

Figure 4. Predicted doubling times for the GEM genomes against measured times for the labeled species.

By habitat, genomes from the built environment habitat group are predicted fastest (median 1.8 hours) and those from wastewater slowest (5.3 hours). The model was never told the habitat. By family, among families with at least 25 genomes the fastest are Thermaceae (0.5 h), Enterobacteriaceae (0.5 h), Sphingobacteriaceae (0.7 h), Streptococcaceae (0.8 h), and the slowest are Gemmataceae (7 h), UBA3495 (7 h), SG8-4 (7 h), Hyphomonadaceae (6 h). Thermaceae grow hot, so their short time is at their own high temperature, and the 20 C column ranks them differently.

What to keep in mind

  • These are statistical estimates, not measurements, and are best used for ranking and for finding groups.
  • The growth rates used for training are species averages from the literature and are not matched to the exact strain that was sequenced. Growth rate can differ a lot between strains of one species.
  • Labeled species are cultured organisms, and the genomes here are mostly uncultured. Predictions for lineages far from anything cultured rest on weaker evidence, which the table's nearest-relative column reflects.
  • The training values are minimal doubling times under good lab conditions, not growth in the environment.
  • Genomes assembled from the environment lose some repeated genes, so rRNA counts and sometimes tRNA counts are underestimated, and incomplete genomes miss ribosomal proteins. We checked the pipeline on real GEM genomes of known species, but only for the 48 species that have them.
  • We did not rerun the comparison with the published tools Phydon and gRodon on the final data.
